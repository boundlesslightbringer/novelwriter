# Conflict Layering

## Identity

**Purpose:** Diagnose whether the conflict in a drafted scene is doing real work — creating momentum, revealing character, or advancing the story's stakes. The question is never "how many conflict levels are present?" It is always "is the conflict that is present earning the scene's place in the narrative?" A scene with one devastating conflict is stronger than a scene with five thin ones. This audit reads for weight, not coverage.

---

## Core

Audit a drafted scene or chapter for the weight and effectiveness of its conflict, not for the number of conflict levels it contains. Assess whether what is present is pulling its narrative weight — generating momentum, revealing character under pressure, or advancing stakes. Identify what is genuinely absent or underused only when its absence is causing a specific, diagnosable weakness. Flag when conflict is present in name but not in force. Output a conflict audit report. Does not rewrite the scene.

---

## The Central Principle

A story's conflict landscape is not a checklist. Scenes serve different purposes at different moments: some are slow burns where a single internal conflict carries an entire chapter; some are pivots where interpersonal and factional pressure land simultaneously; some are deliberately quiet, with the conflict operating entirely beneath the surface. None of these is wrong by virtue of what it is.

**The question is not: does this scene have five kinds of conflict?**  
**The question is: does this scene have enough conflict weight to earn its place?**

The five conflict levels — internal, interpersonal, societal, factional, existential — are a diagnostic vocabulary, not a quota. They exist to help name what is present and what is missing. They do not exist to generate a target count. A scene that uses every level superficially is worse than a scene that uses one level at full depth.

The corollary: **forcing conflict into a scene that doesn't need it produces a predictable, mechanical narrative.** Every quiet scene cannot be made louder. Every functional scene cannot be made fraught. The audit must be capable of returning a verdict of "this scene is doing what it needs to do" — and meaning it.

## Input

The main agent provides:

1. **Drafted scene or chapter** — the prose draft from Scene Construction, passed in for audit. May also be invoked on a scene that feels structurally flat for reasons the main agent cannot yet identify.

2. **World Bible excerpt — Module 1.1 (Character Directory):** Current state of the POV character — emotional state, active desires, active fears, arc stage, beliefs, physical state. Conflict is always assessed relative to who this specific character is and what they are currently experiencing.

3. **World Bible excerpt — Module 1.3 (Relationship Records):** Relevant relationship records for characters who appear in the scene. Interpersonal conflict is assessed against this record — not against what a generic "scene with these two characters" might contain, but against what this specific relationship, at this specific stage of its trajectory, actually holds.

4. *(Optional)* **Scene's intended function** — a brief note from the main agent on what role this scene is supposed to play in the narrative (e.g. "this is a quiet scene meant to establish character in a lull between action beats"; "this is supposed to be the emotional turning point of the chapter"; "this scene is transitional — moving the character from location A to location B"). When provided, the audit calibrates its weight assessment to the scene's intended function rather than applying a single standard to all scenes.

> **Note:** This skill receives excerpts from the main agent. It does not access the World Bible directly.

## Output

**Conflict audit report** — a structured document containing:

### 1. Scene Header and Momentum Verdict

- POV character, scene location, brief scene summary (one sentence).
- **Momentum verdict:** The primary output — a single assessment of whether the scene's conflict is earning its place:
  - `EARNING ITS PLACE` — the conflict present, at whatever level and quantity, is generating sufficient momentum, revelation, or forward pull to justify the scene. No additions are needed; suggestions below are optional.
  - `UNDERLOADED` — the scene contains conflict but it is not landing with enough force. Something is thinning the conflict — it may be present only on the surface, not felt as real pressure; or the most available conflict is being left unused while a less powerful form carries the scene alone.
  - `CARRYING DEAD WEIGHT` — the scene contains conflict, but the conflict is mechanical or manufactured — present because a conflict should be present, not because it arises naturally from who the character is and what the situation demands. This often produces a scene that is technically busy but emotionally inert.
  - `SCENE PURPOSE QUESTION` — the scene has so little conflict weight that the Scene Purpose Test may not be passing: it is neither advancing plot nor revealing character in a way that requires this scene's existence. This verdict should be rare; it is not a default for quiet scenes.

### 2. Conflict Present — What Is Working

Before diagnosing weakness, name what is working. What conflict is present in the scene, and how effectively is it landing? Be specific:
- Which level(s) is active?
- Is it operating at the surface (declared in narration or dialogue) or at depth (felt as real pressure, shaping the character's choices and perceptions)?
- Is it internal to the scene — something the POV character is experiencing right now — or is it ambient, carried as background weight rather than active force?

Surface conflict is not automatically weak. A character explaining their situation to another character can carry strong interpersonal conflict if both characters have something to conceal and something to discover. Surface conflict is weak when it is stated but not felt — when the narration tells the reader that tension exists without creating the experience of tension.

### 3. Conflict Absent or Underused — What Is Missing and Why It Matters

This section exists only when something is missing that would make a material difference to the scene. It is not a catalogue of every conflict level the scene is not using. It is a diagnosis of a specific weakness:

For each weakness identified:
- **What is absent or underused** — which level, and specifically what form of it.
- **Why it matters here** — not "because this level should be present," but because its absence is creating a specific, named problem: the scene is moving too slowly; the character's psychology is not being tested; the reader has no new understanding of who this person is; the stakes are not registering; the scene is not changing anything.
- **What is available** — what material already exists in the scene, in the character's Module 1.1 record, or in the relationship's Module 1.3 record that could carry the missing conflict without requiring new plot events.

If nothing is materially missing — if the scene is carrying its weight with the conflict it has — this section either reads "Nothing materially missing" or is omitted entirely.

### 4. Conflict Weight Assessment — The Five Levels as Diagnostic Tool

This section uses the five levels as a vocabulary for what was found, not as a scorecard. For each level, note whether it is active, latent, absent, or not applicable — and assess whether its current state is appropriate for this scene's purpose and position in the narrative.

| Level | Scope | Diagnostic question |
|-------|-------|---------------------|
| **Internal** | Within the POV character | Is this character's psychology under any real pressure in this scene — or are they operating on autopilot? |
| **Interpersonal** | Between POV character and others | Are any of the relationships in this scene generating active tension — or are characters simply exchanging information? |
| **Societal** | Character vs. social structures/norms | Is the character's social position, its demands, or its constraints doing any work in this scene? |
| **Factional** | Character vs. power structures | Are the political and institutional forces relevant to this character registering in the scene? |
| **Existential** | Character vs. fundamental conditions | Is the scene touching any of the story's largest questions through this character's specific experience? |

For each level, the assessment is a brief diagnostic note — one or two sentences — not a full level-by-level breakdown. The depth of coverage should match the weight of the finding: a level that is working well deserves a sentence of confirmation; a level that is the source of the scene's weakness deserves the detailed treatment in Section 3.

**Important:** A level marked `absent` is not automatically a problem. Mark it as `absent — not applicable` when the scene's material and purpose do not call for it. Mark it as `absent — available` only when its absence is contributing to the weakness identified in Section 3.

### 5. Suggestions *(only when the momentum verdict is not EARNING ITS PLACE)*

If the momentum verdict is EARNING ITS PLACE, this section may be omitted or may contain optional observations — enhancements, not fixes.

If the verdict is UNDERLOADED, CARRYING DEAD WEIGHT, or SCENE PURPOSE QUESTION, this section provides specific, actionable suggestions:

- Each suggestion addresses the specific weakness identified in Section 3.
- Each suggestion must use material already available in the scene and in the character's psychology — no new plot events.
- Each suggestion is stated as: *"[Specific addition or change] — derived from [the character's specific psychology or the scene's existing material] — would [specific effect on the conflict's weight]."*
- Suggestions are ranked. The first suggestion is the one that would most improve the scene with the least disruption to what is already working.

The number of suggestions is proportional to the severity of the weakness. An UNDERLOADED scene typically needs one or two targeted suggestions. A SCENE PURPOSE QUESTION verdict may require a structural conversation rather than suggestions — in which case, note that the issue may be pre-Scene Construction rather than post.

### 6. Scene Rhythm Note *(optional)*

If the scene's conflict weight is appropriate but its rhythm — the pacing of how the conflict lands, escalates, or recedes within the scene — is creating a problem (e.g. the conflict is present but front-loaded so the scene loses momentum before it ends; or the conflict arrives too late and the scene drifts before it finds its tension), note this briefly. This is distinct from the Pacing skill's domain, which operates at the level of psychological state and sentence rhythm. This is a structural observation about how the conflict is sequenced within the scene.

## Conflict Level Reference

| Level | Active when | Weak when | Not applicable when |
|-------|-------------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Internal** | The character's psychology is under genuine pressure — a self-deception straining, a core contradiction surfacing, an emotion suppressed and pressing | It is declared ("he felt torn") but not dramatised — the narration states conflict without the reader experiencing it | The scene is a deliberate pause in which the character is not being tested — and the pause is structurally justified |
| **Interpersonal** | Two characters in the scene have incompatible aims, asymmetric knowledge, or unresolved relational material that is actively shaping the interaction | Characters exchange information or dialogue without the relational subtext doing any work | The scene involves only the POV character, or all present characters are genuinely aligned with no concealed tension |
| **Societal** | The social order — its codes, expectations, or structures — is making a demand on the character that conflicts with who they are or what they want | The social context is present as scenery but not as pressure | The scene occurs outside any social structure or in a context where societal norms are irrelevant to the scene's material |
| **Factional** | A faction's interests, obligations, or power are actively shaping what the character can do, must do, or is doing against their own interest | The faction exists in the world but is not registering in the scene | The scene is genuinely private — no factional forces are present or relevant |
| **Existential** | The scene is touching one of the story's largest questions through this character's specific, lived experience | It is gestured at in narration ("she wondered what it all meant") without the character actually confronting anything | The scene is not at a structural moment that warrants this level — and most scenes are not |

## Boundary

- **Does not** prescribe a minimum or maximum number of conflict levels per scene. Weight is the criterion, not count.
- **Does not** flag absent levels as deficiencies unless their absence is causing a specific, diagnosable problem.
- **Does not** rewrite the scene. Suggests only; Scene Construction executes if the main agent decides to revise.
- **Does not** operate on scenes before they are drafted. The Scene Purpose Test (does this scene need to exist at all?) is a pre-construction question; this audit is post-construction.
- **Does not** audit POV voice consistency, knowledge violations, or prose quality. Those are POV Consistency Maintenance and Self-Editing's domains.
- **Does not** update the World Bible. Output is an audit report returned to the main agent.
- **Does not** mandate revision. A verdict of EARNING ITS PLACE means the scene passes. The main agent may still choose to act on optional observations.

---

## Invocation

The main agent invokes Conflict Layering:

- When the story feels too linear, too predictable, or structurally flat — specifically when a scene or chapter is passing the minimum bar but not generating the tension or momentum that it should.
- After Scene Construction, as an optional quality check on scenes the main agent suspects are underloaded.
- When a scene involves a significant character interaction or turning point, and the main agent wants to verify that the conflict is landing with appropriate weight.
- When a scene feels somehow inert despite containing the right events — when the mechanics are correct but the tension is not present.

Conflict Layering is **not** a mandatory post-construction step for every scene. It is invoked when there is a specific concern about a scene's conflict weight. Routine scenes that are clearly doing their work do not require this audit.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Establish the scene's purpose and expected weight

Before reading the draft for conflict, establish what the scene is trying to do:
- What is its function in the narrative? (If the main agent provided this, use it. If not, infer it from context: the scene's position in the chapter sequence, what the Gardener Plotting System parameters specified, and what the last scene produced.)
- What weight of conflict is appropriate for this function? A transitional scene moving a character between locations has a different conflict requirement than a pivot scene where a long-building tension finally breaks. A quiet character scene in the aftermath of crisis serves a different purpose than a confrontation.

Hold this context throughout the audit. A scene that is doing exactly what it needs to do — even if it carries little overt conflict — may be earning its place.

### Step 2 — Read the draft for what is present and what is landing

Read the scene draft. As you read, track:
- What conflict is present — at which level, in what form?
- Is it landing — felt as pressure, shaping the character's choices and perceptions — or is it declared without being dramatised?
- Where in the scene does the conflict have the most force? Where does it thin or disappear?
- Is the conflict arising from who the character is and what the situation demands — or does it feel installed?

Distinguish between conflict that is present and working, conflict that is present but weak, and conflict that is present but manufactured.

### Step 3 — Assess momentum

Ask the central question: **Is this scene generating enough narrative momentum — enough forward pull, enough revelation, enough escalation of stakes — to justify its existence?**

This is the Scene Purpose Test applied to conflict weight specifically. A scene passes if:
- It is advancing plot (causing things to change in the story's state), **and/or**
- It is revealing character (showing something about who the POV character is under pressure that the reader did not fully have before), **and/or**
- It is building stakes (increasing the reader's sense of what matters and what is at risk)

— and the conflict present is what is producing at least one of these effects with real force.

A scene fails this test when:
- The events occur but nothing changes — the scene could be summarised as "nothing happened" without losing anything.
- The character is present but not under any pressure — they move through the scene unchanged, untested, unilluminated.
- The conflict is declared but not felt — the narration asserts tension that the reader does not experience.

Assign the momentum verdict: EARNING ITS PLACE / UNDERLOADED / CARRYING DEAD WEIGHT / SCENE PURPOSE QUESTION.

### Step 4 — If the verdict is not EARNING ITS PLACE: diagnose the specific weakness

Identify precisely what is causing the scene to fall short. Avoid generic diagnoses ("the internal conflict is weak"). Find the specific diagnosis:
- What material is available — in the character's psychology, in the relationship records, in the scene's existing events — that could carry the missing weight?
- What is preventing that material from doing so? Is it absent entirely? Present but suppressed? Present but stated rather than dramatised?
- Is the weakness at the level of what happens (the events in the scene are not generating conflict) or at the level of how it is written (the events could generate conflict but the prose is not accessing it)?

The second type of weakness — present but not dramatised — is the most common and the most fixable. A Chekhov's gun visible on the wall is not conflict; a character noticing the gun and deciding not to mention they noticed it is.

### Step 5 — Map the five levels as diagnostic vocabulary

For each level, note its status and whether that status is appropriate. Keep this section brief — it is a map of the terrain, not the analysis. The analysis is in Steps 3 and 4.

If a level is absent and the scene would not benefit from it: `absent — not applicable.`  
If a level is absent and the scene is suffering for it: `absent — available` (this will already be covered in Step 4's diagnosis; reference it here).  
If a level is present and working: a sentence confirming what form it takes.  
If a level is present but thin: a brief note, with the detailed diagnosis in Step 4.

### Step 6 — Write suggestions (only if warranted)

If the verdict is not EARNING ITS PLACE, write targeted suggestions. Each suggestion:
- Addresses the specific weakness from Step 4.
- Uses material from the existing scene and character psychology — no new plot events.
- States the specific addition (a thought, a physical detail, a beat of subtext in dialogue) and its predicted effect.
- Is ranked: first suggestion first, in order of impact-to-disruption ratio.

If the verdict is EARNING ITS PLACE, suggestions are optional. If there are genuine enhancement opportunities, note them lightly — not as fixes but as observations the main agent may or may not find useful.

### Step 7 — Add scene rhythm note if warranted

If the conflict weight is appropriate but its sequencing within the scene is creating a problem — it front-loads and the scene loses momentum; it arrives too late; it escalates and then dissipates without landing — add a brief note. This is not a rewrite note; it is a sequencing observation.

### Step 8 — Return the audit report

Return the complete report to the main agent.

## Quality Checks

Before returning output, verify:

- [ ] Momentum verdict is stated explicitly — EARNING ITS PLACE / UNDERLOADED / CARRYING DEAD WEIGHT / SCENE PURPOSE QUESTION.
- [ ] What is working is named before diagnosing weakness — the audit does not lead with deficiency.
- [ ] Absent levels are not flagged as deficiencies unless their absence is causing a specific, named problem.
- [ ] Every diagnosis in Section 3 names the specific weakness and explains why it matters here — not "this level is missing" but "this level is missing and it is causing [specific problem]."
- [ ] Suggestions (if present) use only existing scene material and character psychology — no new plot events.
- [ ] Suggestions are ranked.
- [ ] If the verdict is EARNING ITS PLACE: the audit affirms this clearly and does not undermine the verdict with excessive caveats.
- [ ] No prose rewritten. No plot decisions made.
- [ ] No World Bible writes specified.
