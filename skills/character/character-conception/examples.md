## Example

### Input shape — Early invocation mode

```
Character slot sketch (from Defining Rough Endpoints Pass 1):
  Narrative role: The person whose choice at Milestone 1 defines the story's central question.
  Implied contradiction: Was made into something by someone who loved him / has become something that may have no use for love.
  Stakes: Legitimacy, self-narrative, capacity to be someone worth surviving as.
  World context: Young king, eleven years of regency shaped by his mother; now in personal rule; every faction's loyalty contingent. [From endpoint compass — world not yet built.]
  Open questions:
    1. Does he believe the execution is necessary, or does he need to believe it?
    2. What would refusal mean for him psychologically?
    3. What does he think his mother wants from him in this moment?

Genre/themes: Fantasy / Political Fantasy / Dark Fantasy. Themes: legitimacy and the cost of inheritance (interdependent with) the gap between what we make and what we become.
```

### Output shape — Early invocation mode

```
Role: The king — the person whose choice determines the story's central moral question.

Method: Role-First. The role requires someone who has been functioning as king long enough to believe he is one, but not long enough to have faced a test that separates what he was made into from what he actually is. Worked inward from the specific pressure of that position.

Core contradiction: Genuinely believes in the necessity of ruthlessness as governance / has never stopped waiting, beneath everything, for someone to tell him he has done the right thing.

Sketch: There is something almost formal about the way the king carries himself in the throne room — not stiffness, but the careful stillness of someone who learned very young that how you hold your body is a political statement. He is competent in the ways that are visible: he listens, he reads the room, he makes decisions. He has not yet faced a decision that cost him something that could not be replaced. The court reads him as cold. He is not cold. He is waiting for the moment when the machinery his mother built will stop running and he will find out who is inside it.

Open questions:
  1. What did the regency actually do to him — what specific thing was asked of him, during those eleven years, that he has never told anyone?
  2. What does he believe about his mother that is true? What does he believe that is false?
  3. Is there a version of the execution he could live with? Is there a version that would break him?
  4. Who in the court actually sees him — and what does that person want from him?
  5. What would he do if the execution were unnecessary — if the political threat turned out to be manageable without it?

Provisional flag (early invocation mode):
  World-context assumptions made:
    - The regency was functional, not contested — if it turns out Worldbuilding establishes the regency as a period of factional warfare rather than effective rule, the king's self-understanding (competent, effective, making decisions) will need revision.
    - "Every faction's loyalty is contingent" is treated as the king's accurate read of his situation — if Worldbuilding establishes that one faction is genuinely loyal, the king's isolation is different in nature.
  Most likely to change: The specific nature of what the regency demanded of him (Question 1 above) depends heavily on the political structure Worldbuilding will establish. This should be reviewed once Module 3.1 is populated.
  Key information needed: What is the source of the court's factional division? Is it dynastic, geographic, religious, or economic? The answer changes what the execution means politically — and therefore what it means for the king to order it.
```
